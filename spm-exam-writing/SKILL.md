# ACCT90010 SPM Exam Writing

Turns a question about managerial control systems (Topic 1-5) into an exam-ready English answer, following the writing conventions and content map already distilled from this course's lectures, workshops and case studies. Not a generic "explain the concept" tool — the point is to produce answers that would actually score well on SPM's discussion-heavy final (60% of the grade, mostly essay-style, small calculation component).

## Step 1: Identify the command word before drafting anything

Read the question and circle the verb first — it decides the structure of the whole answer, independent of content.

| Command word | What it requires |
|---|---|
| Explain | "What + why," no need to judge good/bad |
| Outline | Brief list of points, no deep justification |
| Discuss | Multiple angles, usually pros and cons, balanced |
| Evaluate / Critically evaluate | Weigh strengths/weaknesses AND reach a judgement — not just a list |
| Compare and contrast | Similarities AND differences, ideally paired |
| Recommend / Advise | Clear recommendation with reasoning, not just analysis |
| Justify | Persuasive reasons for a stated position |
| Analyse the behavioural implications of... | How the practice/metric will change managers' decisions/behaviour (goal congruence, dysfunctional behaviour) — not just financial effects |

Ask: does this question need a final judgement/recommendation? Almost all SPM discussion questions do — a list of pros and cons with no conclusion under-scores even with correct content.

## Step 2: Draft every paragraph as P-A-J-L

One argument per paragraph, in this order:

1. **Point** — one sentence stating the argument/concept.
2. **Apply** — tie it to the specific company/case/numbers in the question. This is where most marks are lost: theory that never touches the case facts caps out around a pass.
3. **Justify** — explain the underlying mechanism/theory (controllability principle, goal congruence, opportunity cost, etc.).
4. **Link** — connect back to the question or forward to the next paragraph ("so what" for the final recommendation).

Example (Kranworth, structure change):
> **Point:** Moving from a functional to a divisional structure fundamentally changed the basis on which Kranworth's managers were held accountable.
> **Apply:** Under the new structure, Joseph (Retail) and Ed (Custom) are evaluated as investment centre managers using controllable return, rather than assessed on functional efficiency as before.
> **Justify:** This reflects the controllability principle — divisional managers now hold decision rights over pricing, product mix and (to some extent) capital investment, so it is appropriate their performance measure captures profit relative to the assets they control.
> **Link:** This makes the design of controllable assets (e.g. how R&D allocations are treated) critical, since holding managers accountable for costs they cannot influence would undermine goal congruence.

## Step 3: Pick the template that matches the question type

- **Structure / responsibility-centre design** ("benefits and shortcomings of a divisional structure?"): one-line summary of the decision-rights shift → benefits (3-4, each P-A-J-L) → shortcomings (3-4) → recommendation/hybrid if asked. Vocabulary: decision rights, local knowledge, controllability principle, accountability, silo effect, goal congruence, span of control.
- **Performance-metric evaluation** ("evaluate ROI as a measure for X"): state the relevant suitability criteria (pick 3-4 of the 7 below) → assess the metric against each in the case's context → discuss behavioural implications → recommend (keep / redefine / combine with RI or non-financial measures).
- **Calculation + interpretation** (ROI/RI/CR, transfer-pricing numbers): show workings with the formula stated first → state what the number means in one sentence → interpret against the metric's known limitations (don't just report the number) → give an explicit final recommendation if asked (invest/bonus/etc.), never stop at the arithmetic.
- **Transfer pricing recommendation**: calculate the price under each method named in the question → for each, ask (a) does it serve the company as a whole? (b) will both divisions accept it (goal congruence at divisional level)? → name where methods conflict → recommend based on strategy (cooperative vs competitive), idle capacity, and sourcing autonomy.
- **Risk identification** ("categorise these risks and suggest mitigations"): classify into strategic / operational / regulatory / financial with case evidence for each → 2-3 concrete mitigation techniques per category (not generic "improve risk management") → if asked, note the consequences of a poorly designed control system itself (misallocation of resources, unreliable pricing, dysfunctional behaviour).

## Step 4: Reusable sentence stems (by function)

- Define: "X refers to the process/practice of…" / "At its core, X is concerned with…"
- Apply to case: "In the case of [Company/Division], this is evident from…" / "Applying this to [Company]'s situation,…"
- Benefit: "The main benefit of X is that…" / "X is particularly effective in this context because…"
- Drawback/risk: "However, a potential drawback of X is that…" / "This creates the risk that managers will…"
- Comparison: "In contrast to X, Y places greater emphasis on…" / "The key trade-off here is between [goal A] and [goal B]."
- Behavioural implications: "This is likely to create an incentive for managers to…" / "This could give rise to dysfunctional decision-making, such as…" / "From an agency theory perspective, this reflects a potential misalignment between the interests of [division] and [head office/the firm as a whole]."
- Recommendation: "Given the above analysis, it is recommended that…" / "On balance, X would be the more appropriate approach because…"
- Open-endedness (many SPM questions have no single right answer — use this to sound deliberate, not indecisive): "There is no single correct answer here; rather, the appropriate choice depends on…"
- Transitions: "Building on this point,…" / "A related but distinct issue is…" / "Turning now to…"

## Step 5: Self-check before submitting (common ways SPM answers lose marks)

- Every theory point is tied to a case fact/number — no paragraph is pure theory.
- Every paragraph opens with a clear topic sentence (the Point).
- No direct-translation-style vague phrasing ("this can bring some benefits to the company") — use specific outcomes instead.
- Tense is consistent: case facts in past/simple present, general theory statements in simple present, not mixed within a paragraph.
- Evaluate/Recommend questions end with an actual stance, even a conditional one.
- Full term given on first use with abbreviation after ("return on investment (ROI)"), then abbreviation used consistently.
- Terms used precisely, not swapped: opportunity cost vs sunk cost, controllable vs uncontrollable assets, etc.

## Content map: Topic 1-5 (the spine every essay should be able to trace)

Strategy -> Organisational structure -> Decision rights -> Responsibility centres -> Performance measures -> Rewards -> Managerial behaviour. Almost any discussion question can be angled through this chain: structure changed -> responsibility centres changed -> should the metric change -> could it induce dysfunctional behaviour -> what adjustment follows.

**Topic 1 — Introduction to MCS.** Management accounting now covers external/non-financial/predictive/behavioural information, not just bookkeeping. MCS purpose (5 points): monitor the whole value chain; give timely reliable decision info; flag opportunities/threats needing strategy review; conserve management attention; shape behaviour positively. Five frameworks exist; **Flamholtz's control system framework** is the one used through this course. Risk classification: strategic (competitive advantage), operational (delivery of goods/services), regulatory (compliance), financial (solvency) — know 3-4 mitigation techniques per category. Key closing idea: control systems must be customised to each organisation — there is no single correct answer, which is exactly why SPM essays are open-ended. Cases: Netflix (strategy shift -> MCS shift, risk classification practice), Books by Alex (single divisional-ROI metric -> weak cross-division cooperation), Wells Fargo (cross-sell targets -> fake accounts, classic unintended-consequences example).

**Topic 2 — Organisational structure & responsibility accounting (Kranworth).** Structure is "the foundation" of any MCS. Three responsibility centres: cost centre (input-mix decisions, minimise cost), profit centre (+ price/mix decisions, maximise profit), investment centre (+ capital investment decisions, maximise ROI/RI). Logic chain: local knowledge -> allocation of decision rights -> performance measures and rewards. Structural options: functional, divisional, matrix, hybrid. Silo effect (Tett 2015): aids order/accountability but causes tunnel vision; countered by formal (redesign metrics/structure, cross-functional teams) and informal (idea brokers, induction camps — e.g. Spotify) mechanisms. Core principle: accountability should match structure/authority (controllability principle) — never hold a manager accountable for what they can't influence. Kranworth facts: functional -> Retail (high-volume, low-margin) + Custom (low-volume, high-margin) divisions as profit centres; R&D/engineering/supply chain/HR stayed centralised, creating tension with divisional accountability; new metric = Controllable return = Operating income before tax / Controllable assets; bonus split 75% divisional/25% corporate for division managers, 100% corporate for HQ managers. Part 2 (Week 5 workshop) adds ratchet-style target setting and Project Upgrade (low ROI years 1-2, higher 5-year average ROI, positive NPV) — the standard ROI-short-termism-vs-RI/NPV trap.

**Topic 3 — Long-term assets (PPE & intangibles, AASB 116/IAS 16).** PPE: recognise if probable future economic benefits + reliably measurable; initial cost = purchase price + directly attributable costs + initial dismantling/restoration estimate; subsequent expenditure is expensed if it merely maintains normal operation, capitalised if it increases capacity/quality/life; depreciation methods: straight-line, diminishing balance, units of production. Intangibles: identifiable, non-monetary, no physical substance; internally generated brands/customer lists usually fail reliable-measurement -> not recognised; research phase always expensed, development phase capitalised only if all 6 criteria met (technical feasibility, intention and ability to complete/use/sell, resources available, reliable cost measurement, ability to demonstrate future benefit); finite-life intangibles amortised, indefinite-life tested annually for impairment instead. Exam angle: capitalise-vs-expense judgement calls, and linking depreciation/amortisation policy to a division's controllable-asset base and ROI.

**Topic 4 — Measuring sub-unit performance (ROI & RI).** 7 suitability criteria for a performance metric: reflects strategy/key drivers; reflects managerial responsibility; responsive to managerial action; has intended behavioural effects (not gameable); faithfully represents what it claims to; comparable/timely/verifiable/understandable; cost-effective. ROI = divisional profit / divisional investment — simple and comparable, but causes the **under-investment problem**: a manager rejects a project earning above the firm's cost of capital but below the division's current average ROI (goal incongruence). Six fixes: combine with other metrics; redefine components; change the measurement horizon; align HQ/division incentives; manage tenure; use RI or EVA instead. RI = divisional profit − (required rate of return × divisional investment) — accepts any project above the hurdle rate, but is an absolute number so it's harder to compare across differently sized divisions. Answer structure for "should we use a single metric?" questions: benefits of simplicity -> limitations (short-termism, gaming) -> case evidence -> recommendation (combine metrics / change definition / change horizon / add RI or non-financial measures).

**Topic 5 — Transfer pricing (Goliath, Birch Paper).** Transfer pricing is effectively a make-or-buy decision delegated to divisional managers, who may optimise their own unit's result rather than firm value -> potential goal incongruence. Four methods: cost-based (variable or full cost — suits cooperative-style organisations), market-based (suits competitive-style organisations), negotiated (suits units treated as independent), dual-price (buyer and seller use different prices). Key calculation: transfer price = variable cost + forgone contribution margin (opportunity cost) — near variable cost if idle capacity exists, plus the lost margin if internal transfer displaces external sales. Company-level decision framework: (1) is the current responsibility-centre design optimal given interdependencies? (2) should sourcing be centralised or decentralised? (3) which pricing method + how much sourcing/selling autonomy? Goliath case: three investment-centre divisions, four methods with case-specific definitions (must use the question's exact definitions, not textbook generic ones) — first judge whether the internal transfer should happen at all (internal cost vs external market price) at the whole-company level, then test each method for whether both divisions would actually agree to trade. Birch Paper case: full-cost-plus quote versus lower external quotes, seller has idle capacity but still prices at full cost + standard markup, ignoring true opportunity cost; the case's key insight is that a cost variable at the divisional level can be fixed at the company level in the short run — central to arguing which quote maximises firm profit and whether headquarters should intervene. Standard three-part answer structure for transfer-pricing case questions: **Calculation -> Evaluation (whose interest does this serve — divisional vs company as a whole) -> Recommendation (policy design)**, using the terms goal congruence, sourcing autonomy, opportunity cost, and idle capacity explicitly (these are scored keywords).

## Guardrails

- Never produce a "list of pros and cons" answer for Evaluate/Recommend/Discuss questions without a closing judgement — SPM's marking expects a stance, even a conditional one.
- Never discuss a theory point without tying it to the specific case's numbers or facts within the same paragraph.
- For calculation questions, always show the formula and the substitution, not just the final figure — workings carry marks independent of the final answer.
- When case-specific method definitions are given in the question (e.g. Goliath's four transfer-pricing definitions), use those exact definitions rather than generic textbook versions.
- Flag when a question seems to span topics (e.g. divisional structure change affecting controllable assets under Topic 3) so the answer pulls from both areas rather than treating them as isolated.

## Output

A structured English draft answer (or outline, if asked for less) using P-A-J-L paragraphs, the command-word-appropriate template from Step 3, sentence stems from Step 4, and — for calculation questions — explicit workings followed by interpretation and a recommendation.
