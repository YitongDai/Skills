# Consulting Workplace Skills

The "soft" side of running a short, deliverable-driven engagement — worth capturing separately because these skills transfer to almost any future placement, internship, or junior consulting role, regardless of the specific analytical content.

## 1. Working within someone else's institutional constraints

Every client organisation comes with its own fixed constraints — a required file storage system, a required output format (e.g. a specific presentation tool, not whatever's most convenient), and process gates (like ethics clearance) that can't be skipped even under time pressure. Treat these as fixed inputs to plan around from day one, not obstacles to negotiate away. Confirm them explicitly early rather than discovering them mid-engagement.

## 2. Splitting work across a small team without losing coherence

When a handful of people are each covering different stages or sections of one deliverable:

- Get explicit agreement on who owns which stage/section before starting, and keep a shared reference of that ownership (even a single shared doc) so nobody duplicates or drops something.
- When your section depends on another teammate's earlier work (their data pull, their template, their draft), treat their output as provisional until you've sanity-checked it yourself — don't propagate someone else's labelling error (see the multiplier-ratio trap) three steps downstream just because it came from a teammate.
- Keep the client contact and any academic/managerial supervisor's respective concerns visible and distinct — a client cares about the deliverable's usefulness to them; a supervisor may also be assessing process, documentation, or a specific rubric. Don't assume the same update satisfies both.

## 3. Communicating with a non-technical or time-poor sponsor

- Lead findings with the concrete number or comparison, not the method used to get there — a sponsor deciding how to use the work needs the "what," and will ask for the "how" if they want it.
- When a finding is uncomfortable (a funding drop, a missing data relationship, a limitation in their flagship case study), still surface it — a sponsor advocating externally with an evidence base that quietly hides its own weak points is more exposed later than one who knows where the soft spots are.
- Match deliverable format to what they'll actually use: if a live presentation is the real deliverable, don't over-invest in a written report nobody will read closely, and vice versa.

## 4. Making and documenting scope decisions

Time-boxed engagements always surface more interesting tangents than can be pursued. The skill isn't avoiding these — it's deciding fast and explicitly:

- When a genuinely secondary question comes up (a definitional ambiguity, a data source that's out of scope, a methodology refinement that wouldn't change the conclusion), make an explicit call to defer it, log it somewhere visible, and keep moving.
- Don't silently let a deferred item drop out of memory — if it resurfaces later (e.g. someone asks about it, or it turns out to matter more than expected), you should be able to point to exactly when and why it was deferred.

## 5. Iterating on deliverables efficiently

- Once a section or figure has been reviewed and approved, treat it as locked. Build the next piece on top of it rather than reopening it "while you're in there" — reopening approved work without being asked erodes trust and wastes review cycles.
- Prefer targeted revisions (a specific paragraph, a specific chart) over wholesale rewrites when incorporating feedback — it's easier for a reviewer to spot what changed, and less likely to accidentally undo something they'd already approved.

## 6. Framing the experience afterwards

When turning this kind of engagement into a CV bullet point, LinkedIn update, or interview story (e.g. for competitive graduate recruitment):

- Emphasise **end-to-end analytical ownership** — not just "did analysis" but the shape of the work: multi-source data integration, methodology decisions made under real data limitations, and a real external stakeholder deliverable with a deadline.
- Name the *kind* of judgment calls made (e.g. "identified and corrected a data mislabeling error that would have understated X" or "flagged an evidence gap rather than estimating around it") rather than only the tools used — this is what distinguishes real analytical experience from a tools list.
- Be precise about scope: describe the specific stages/sections actually owned, not the whole team's output, since this will likely come up in follow-up interview questions.
