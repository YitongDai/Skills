# Advocacy Evidence Synthesis

How to turn integrated data (see `economic-impact-methodology.md`) plus existing organisational materials into a case study, gap analysis, and forward-looking data roadmap that a client can actually use for advocacy or funding conversations.

## 1. Vetting an existing case study before you build on it

Organisations often already have one flagship example they want you to use (a program, a project, a location). Before adopting it:

- Check whether it has **both** economic data (cost, funding, reach) **and** social/qualitative outcome data (wellbeing, community feedback, participation). Many organisational case studies are written for a communications audience and only have one side of this — that's fine for a newsletter, but not sufficient for a defensible advocacy evidence base.
- Check that the time periods actually line up. It's common for cost data to sit in one financial year and impact/outcome data to sit in a different one (e.g. costs incurred the year before an evaluation was published) — if so, this is a real limitation to state plainly, not a gap to quietly bridge with an assumption.
- If the existing case study can't carry the full analytical weight needed, don't force it — either supplement it with a second, smaller example that fills the missing dimension, or scope the case study section down to what the evidence actually supports and say so.

## 2. Reconciling the brief against the data

The people who wrote the original project brief may describe a relationship or arrangement (a funding stream, a partnership, a data source) that turns out not to be visible in the actual records once you look — e.g. a described local-government funding line that doesn't appear anywhere in the general ledger for the year in question.

When this happens:
1. Don't assume the brief is right and the data is incomplete — verify at the most granular level available (e.g. line-item ledger, not summary reports) before concluding there's a discrepancy.
2. Once confirmed, state it as a factual finding, worded neutrally (e.g. "no [X] funding was identified in [year] records" rather than "[X] didn't happen" or accusing anyone of an error) — the brief's authors may simply be describing an earlier or intended arrangement.
3. Flag it to the client contact rather than silently omitting the claim from the deliverable — it may be something they want to correct, chase up, or explain.

## 3. Structuring the data gaps log into a roadmap

A running log of every evidence gap encountered (see `Data_Gaps_Log` in the methodology doc) becomes the backbone of a forward-looking recommendations section. For each gap, capture:

- **What's missing** and why it matters for the client's advocacy case.
- **Why it's currently missing** (not published, not collected, requires primary research, requires a paid data tier, etc.).
- **What it would take to close it** — and critically, whether closing it requires **primary data collection** (staff interviews, surveys, focus groups). If so, flag that formal ethics clearance is very likely required before any such collection can begin, and that this is a lead-time item, not something to schedule casually near the end of an engagement.
- **Priority** — not every gap is worth closing; rank by how much it would strengthen the specific advocacy argument the client cares about most, not by how interesting it is analytically.

## 4. Preparing the client-facing synthesis

- Write findings that reference the actual concrete data points from the engagement (named metrics, specific figures, specific evidence gaps) rather than generic methodology narration — a client reading "we compared several indicators across sub-regions" learns nothing; a client reading the actual comparison with the actual numbers can act on it.
- If the deliverable is a live presentation to a real audience (a CEO, a board, an academic assessor) under a time limit, script to the clock — read the script aloud once and check it against the actual allotted minutes, don't just estimate from word count alone.
- Keep a clear line between "what the data shows" and "what we recommend" — advocacy deliverables are most credible when a reader can see the evidence and the interpretation as separate, traceable steps rather than blended together.
